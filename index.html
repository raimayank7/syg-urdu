    Who this site is for

    It is for the Hindi speaker who hums Urdu words in every film song and would like to read them as they were written. It is for the family whose grandparents wrote in Urdu and whose children cannot read their letters. It is for the student of Hindi literature who meets Urdu at every turn, and for the traveller who wants to read the signboards of Lucknow, Hyderabad or Srinagar. If you can speak Hindi, you are much closer to reading Urdu than you think.

    One language, two scripts

    In everyday speech Hindi and Urdu are the same language with the same grammar. The sentence "आपका नाम क्या है?" is identical in both; in Urdu it is written آپ کا نام کیا ہے؟ and read from the right. That is why every phrase on this site carries a Devanagari line. It is not a translation. It is the same words in the script you already read, so that you can match letter to letter. How the two languages are alike and where they part is explained in Urdu and Hindi.

    Common questions

    Can a Hindi speaker learn to read Urdu quickly?

    Yes. You already know the language, so only the script is new. With about twenty minutes a day, most Hindi speakers can read simple Urdu slowly in around ten days. Fluent reading takes a few months of regular practice.

    Why is Urdu written from right to left?

    Its script comes from the Arabic alphabet by way of Persian, and both are written from right to left. Numbers inside Urdu text, however, are written from left to right.

    Are Urdu and Hindi the same language?

    In everyday speech they are practically the same, with shared grammar and core words. They differ in script and in formal vocabulary: Urdu draws on Persian and Arabic, Hindi on Sanskrit.

    Why are short vowels missing in Urdu writing?

    By convention only long vowels are written. Short a, i and u have marks (zabar, zer, pesh), but these are usually left out except in books for learners. Readers who know the words supply the vowels easily.

    Why do the letters look different on my phone?

    Urdu is traditionally printed in the sloping Nastaliq style. If your device cannot load a Nastaliq font, it shows the same letters in the flatter Naskh style used for Arabic. Both are correct.
