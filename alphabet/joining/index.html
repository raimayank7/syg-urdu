How Urdu letters join

Urdu is always written joined up, in print as well as by hand. There are no capital letters and no separate "printed" style. A letter therefore changes its shape according to where it stands: at the start of a group, in the middle, at the end, or alone.

That sounds like four alphabets, but it is not. In nearly every case the start and middle forms are simply the head of the letter with its tail cut off, so that the next letter can attach. The dots stay exactly the same, and it is the dots that identify the letter. Look at ب in the table: alone it is a long boat with a dot below; at the start of a word only a small tooth with a dot below remains. The dot tells you it is still "b".

Devanagari readers will recognise the idea. In क्या or स्त the first letter loses its vertical line and leans on the next. Urdu does the same thing all the time.

The letters that do not join forward

A few letters join to the letter before them but never to the letter after. After one of them there is always a small gap inside the word, and the next letter begins again in its "start" shape. They are:

ا د ڈ ذ ر ڑ ز ژ و ے

This is why a single word can look like two or three pieces. دروازہ (darwaaza, door) falls into the pieces d, r, w, aa, z-a, because five of its letters refuse to join forward. A beginner often takes the gaps for spaces between words. Real word spaces are a little wider.

Nastaliq: why Urdu slopes downward

Urdu is traditionally written in a style called Nastaliq, in which each group of joined letters starts high on the right and slides down towards the left. Arabic and most computer text use the flatter Naskh style, where letters sit on a level line. The letters are the same; only the look differs. This site shows Urdu in a Nastaliq font where your device can load it, because that is what Urdu books and newspapers look like. In Nastaliq the shapes change more than the neat table above suggests: a letter may sit higher or lower and its tooth may shrink, depending on its neighbours. Do not let that worry you. Count the dots and the teeth.

How to practise

  1. Count teeth and dots. In the middle of a word, ب پ ت ٹ ث ن ی all become the same small tooth. Only the dots differ: one below is b, three below is p, two above is t, one above is n, two below is y.
  2. Write, do not just read. Take five words from everyday words and copy each ten times, right to left, lifting the pen only where a non-joining letter forces you to.
  3. Put the dots last. Write the whole joined shape first, then go back and add the dots and marks, as English writers cross t's and dot i's.
  4. Read shop boards. Signboards are written large and clear, and you usually know what the shop sells.
